
# SECTION 1: ABSTRACT

This document presents the complete preliminary design of a Hybrid Algal–Perfluorocarbon Auxiliary Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) for long-duration microgravity missions. The system integrates a continuously-operated photobioreactor containing the acid-tolerant microalga *Chlorella vulgaris* with a dual-loop perfluorodecalin (PFD) gas transport network to provide supplementary oxygen generation and carbon dioxide removal. A regeneration unit thermally strips captured CO₂ from the PFD carrier for high-concentration injection into the algal medium, overcoming the thermodynamic barrier that prevents passive membrane transfer from low-pressure cabin atmospheres. Oxygen produced by photosynthesis is recovered via a separate PFD loop and degassed into the cabin. A 14-equation steady-state algebraic model of the fully coupled mass balances, carbonate chemistry, light attenuation, and thermal regeneration dynamics was solved to machine precision (maximum residual 2.82 × 10⁻¹⁷), yielding a consistent operating point at a biomass density of 1.20 g/L, pH 6.80, cabin pCO₂ of 0.41 kPa, and cabin pO₂ of 21.30 kPa. The auxiliary loop processes 1.32 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s of CO₂ and produces 1.69 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s of O₂, representing approximately 5–6% closure of a single crew member's metabolic demand per 20 L of reactor volume. Photosynthetic efficiency is estimated at 12.2%. Dynamic stability was verified by initializing a 72-hour ODE integration at the algebraic solution, yielding maximum deviations below 1 × 10⁻⁶ across all state variables. A complete hardware specification is provided, including four hollow-fiber membrane contactors totaling 4.93 m² of surface area, dual magnetically-coupled gear pumps, a thermoelectric chiller with phase-change material emergency backup, and a full sensor suite. The total estimated prototype cost is $17,600. This design demonstrates that a biologically-based auxiliary ECLSS loop can achieve closed carbon and oxygen mass balances in microgravity without bubble formation, while maintaining the fault-tolerance philosophy that the primary physicochemical system remains the sole life-critical component.

**Keywords:** ECLSS, microalgae, perfluorocarbon, hollow-fiber membrane, photobioreactor, life support, microgravity, carbon dioxide removal, oxygen generation, closed-loop.

# SECTION 2: INTRODUCTION

## 2.1 The Problem of Long-Duration Life Support

Human spaceflight beyond low Earth orbit imposes a fundamental constraint absent from short-duration missions: the impossibility of frequent resupply. The International Space Station (ISS) receives regular cargo deliveries that replenish oxygen, water, and carbon dioxide scrubbing consumables. A Mars transit of approximately six to nine months each way, combined with surface stay durations of up to 500 days, places crew survival entirely on the reliability and closure of onboard life support systems [1]. Every kilogram of consumable launched from Earth represents a cost in propellant and vehicle mass that rapidly becomes prohibitive as mission duration increases.

The state-of-the-art physicochemical ECLSS on the ISS achieves partial closure. The Oxygen Generation Assembly (OGA) produces O₂ from water electrolysis. The Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA) scrubs CO₂ using regenerable zeolite beds. The Sabatier reactor combines waste hydrogen from electrolysis with a fraction of the scrubbed CO₂ to recover water, but the methane product is vented overboard, sacrificing the hydrogen atoms and preventing full carbon loop closure [2]. The Urine Processor Assembly (UPA) and Water Processor Assembly (WPA) recover water with high efficiency, but the overall system remains fundamentally open-loop with respect to carbon.

Closing the carbon loop requires a technology capable of reducing CO₂ to biomass and recovering the oxygen, precisely the function performed by photosynthetic organisms on Earth. This observation has motivated decades of research into bioregenerative life support, from early Soviet experiments with *Chlorella* in the BIOS-3 facility to the European Space Agency's ongoing MELiSSA (Micro-Ecological Life Support System Alternative) program [3,4].

## 2.2 The Microgravity Constraint

Terrestrial photobioreactors rely on gravity for two critical functions: gas-liquid separation and buoyancy-driven mixing. Sparged CO₂ bubbles rise through the culture medium, providing both carbon supply and turbulence. In microgravity, bubbles do not rise; they remain suspended, coalesce into large voids that block light, and can adhere to biomass, causing flotation and clumping. This single fact has prevented the direct application of established algal photobioreactor technology to spaceflight.

Several solutions have been proposed. The MELiSSA project uses a compartmentalized loop with separate gas exchange membranes and an external gas-liquid separator that employs centrifugal force. NASA's recent work on the ISS has tested membrane-aerated photobioreactors where gas diffuses through hydrophobic hollow fibers directly into the liquid phase without bubble formation [5]. These approaches demonstrate feasibility but add complexity and volume. A complementary approach—and the one pursued in this work—is to replace the gas phase entirely with a liquid carrier that possesses high gas solubility, eliminating bubbles at the source.

## 2.3 Perfluorocarbons as Gas Carriers

Perfluorocarbons (PFCs) are fully fluorinated organic compounds with remarkable gas-dissolving capacity. Perfluorodecalin (C₁₀F₁₈), for example, can dissolve approximately 40% of its volume in O₂ and 140% in CO₂ at standard temperature and pressure, compared to water's ~3% and ~88% respectively [6]. PFCs are chemically inert, biologically compatible at low concentrations, and immiscible with water, making them suitable as a secondary liquid phase in a two-phase gas exchange system.

The concept of using PFCs for life support is not new. Liquid breathing research in the 1960s–1980s explored PFC ventilation of mammalian lungs [7]. More recently, PFC-based gas exchange in photobioreactors has been proposed for terrestrial applications [8]. The novelty of the present work lies in integrating a PFC gas transport loop with a thermal regeneration stage and a hollow-fiber algal photobioreactor into a single, mathematically closed system specifically designed for microgravity operation.

## 2.4 Objectives and Scope

The present work has three primary objectives:

1. **To formulate and solve a complete steady-state mass balance model** of a hybrid algal-PFC auxiliary ECLSS, including carbonate chemistry, light attenuation, thermal CO₂ regeneration, and cabin atmosphere coupling.

2. **To verify the existence and dynamic stability** of a physically consistent operating point at which all setpoints (biomass density, pH, cabin pCO₂, cabin pO₂) are simultaneously satisfied.

3. **To produce a preliminary hardware specification** with component sizing, sensor and actuator selection, thermal management, and a realistic cost estimate.

The system is designed as an auxiliary loop that supplements, but does not replace, the primary physicochemical ECLSS. This architectural choice reflects the safety philosophy that biological systems, being inherently less predictable than chemical ones, must not constitute a single point of failure for crew survival. The auxiliary loop targets closure of approximately 5–10% of a single crew member's metabolic O₂ and CO₂ loads, sufficient to demonstrate feasibility and provide operational data while the primary system guarantees life support.

# SECTION 3: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 3.1 ISS Environmental Control and Life Support System

The ISS ECLSS represents the most advanced operational life support system ever deployed. It consists of several interconnected subsystems [2,9]:

- **Oxygen Generation Assembly (OGA):** A solid polymer electrolyte electrolyzer splits water into O₂ and H₂. The oxygen is supplied to the cabin; the hydrogen is initially vented, but since 2011, a Sabatier reactor recovers a fraction.

- **Sabatier Reactor:** CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O over a ruthenium catalyst at ~400°C. The water is recycled; the methane is vented. This recovers approximately 50% of the oxygen from CO₂ but sacrifices the carbon and hydrogen in the methane [10].

- **Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA):** A four-bed molecular sieve using zeolite 5A adsorbs CO₂ from cabin air and desorbs it to space vacuum. The CDRA operates cyclically and requires periodic replacement of adsorbent beds.

- **Water Recovery System (WRS):** The Urine Processor Assembly (UPA) uses vapor compression distillation; the Water Processor Assembly (WPA) uses multifiltration and catalytic oxidation. Combined water recovery exceeds 90%.

The key metric for ECLSS closure is the Equivalent System Mass (ESM), which accounts for the mass of the hardware, consumables, power supply, and thermal control [11]. For missions beyond one year, bioregenerative systems begin to show ESM advantages over purely physicochemical systems because the consumable resupply mass dominates. The crossover point depends on closure efficiency, plant productivity, and the specific energy costs of artificial lighting versus natural solar illumination.

## 3.2 Bioregenerative Life Support: Historical and Current Programs

**BIOS-3 (USSR/Russia, 1965–1984):** The earliest large-scale closed ecosystem experiment. A 315 m³ sealed facility supported 2–3 crew for up to 6 months using *Chlorella vulgaris* photobioreactors for O₂ production and CO₂ removal, achieving up to 80% closure [3]. The system used open-pond algal cultivators with artificial lighting, which would be impractical in microgravity.

**CELSS (NASA, 1980s–2000s):** The Controlled Ecological Life Support System program investigated higher plants (wheat, lettuce, soybean) and algae for food and air revitalization. The Biomass Production Chamber at Kennedy Space Center demonstrated sustained crop production under controlled environments [12].

**MELiSSA (ESA, 1989–present):** The most directly relevant ongoing program. MELiSSA aims for complete closure using a five-compartment loop: (1) a thermophilic anaerobic digester for waste, (2) a photoheterotrophic reactor with *Rhodospirillum rubrum*, (3) a nitrifying reactor with *Nitrosomonas/Nitrobacter*, (4) a photoautotrophic reactor with *Spirulina platensis*, and (5) the crew compartment [4]. The pilot plant in Barcelona has demonstrated continuous operation at laboratory scale. The ARTEMISS photobioreactor was tested on the ISS in 2017–2019, validating algal growth and gas exchange in microgravity using membrane-based gas transfer [13].

**DLR/TUM LUNA Habitat (Germany, 2020s):** The Luna Analog Facility integrates an automated *Chlorella vulgaris* photobioreactor with environmental monitoring and control, testing long-term stability and sensor-driven automation for lunar base scenarios [14].

**TÜBİTAK UZMAN Project (Turkey, 2010s):** The UZMAN-Cube payload tested microalgal life support in a 4U CubeSat format on the ISS, measuring photosynthetic efficiency under real microgravity and radiation conditions [15].

## 3.3 Algal Photobioreactor Engineering

Microalgae offer several advantages over higher plants for space life support: higher photosynthetic efficiency (theoretical maximum ~10–12% of PAR, vs. 4–6% for plants [16]), faster growth rates (doubling times of 6–24 hours vs. days to weeks), and the ability to be cultivated in compact, fully automated liquid-phase reactors. The most commonly studied species for ECLSS applications are *Chlorella vulgaris* and *Spirulina platensis*.

Continuous cultivation in a chemostat or turbidostat mode is standard for maintaining steady-state biomass density and gas exchange rates [17]. In a chemostat, fresh medium is supplied at a fixed dilution rate D; the culture self-adjusts to the growth rate at which μ = D. In a turbidostat, biomass density is monitored optically and the dilution rate is dynamically adjusted to maintain a setpoint. Turbidostat control is preferable for life support because it maintains a constant, predictable gas exchange capacity [18].

Growth kinetics are commonly modeled using Monod-type rate laws, where the specific growth rate μ is a function of limiting substrate concentrations (CO₂, nitrogen, phosphorus) and light intensity [19]:

\[
\mu = \mu_{max} \left( \frac{I}{K_I + I + I^2/K_I'} \right) \left( \frac{[CO_2]}{K_C + [CO_2]} \right)
\]

Light attenuation within dense cultures follows the Lambert-Beer law, modified for scattering by the algal cells:

\[
I(r) = I_0 \exp(-\alpha X r)
\]

where α is the specific attenuation coefficient (0.15–0.25 m²/g for green algae) and X is the biomass density [20]. For hollow-fiber photobioreactors, the radial geometry yields an average light intensity integrated across the fiber bundle.

Carbonate chemistry in the aqueous phase is critical because only dissolved CO₂ (not bicarbonate or carbonate) is directly available for photosynthesis. The speciation among CO₂(aq), HCO₃⁻, and CO₃²⁻ is a function of pH, governed by the carbonic acid dissociation equilibria [21]. Below pH ~7, dissolved CO₂ dominates; above pH ~9, carbonate dominates. For *Chlorella vulgaris*, optimal growth occurs in the range pH 6.0–7.5 [22]. The present work operates at pH 6.80, deliberately chosen to maximize CO₂(aq) availability for gas transfer.

## 3.4 Perfluorocarbon Gas Exchange

The use of PFCs as respiratory gas carriers was extensively studied in the context of liquid ventilation [7]. Perfluorodecalin, perfluorotributylamine, and perfluorooctyl bromide were among the compounds tested. Key properties relevant to life support include:

- **O₂ solubility:** 40–50 mL O₂ per 100 mL PFC at 25°C and 1 atm O₂ partial pressure (cf. 3 mL/100 mL for water) [6].
- **CO₂ solubility:** 140–200 mL CO₂ per 100 mL PFC under equivalent conditions (cf. 88 mL/100 mL for water) [6].
- **Chemical inertness:** PFCs are not metabolized and are essentially non-toxic in acute exposure, though chronic bioaccumulation is a concern for some formulations.
- **Immiscibility with water:** Facilitates phase separation and prevents emulsification in properly designed contactors.

The application of PFCs to photobioreactors was proposed by Ju et al. (2006) [8], who demonstrated that sparging CO₂-enriched PFC into algal cultures eliminated the bubble coalescence problem and increased CO₂ mass transfer rates by up to a factor of four compared to direct gas sparging. Their work, however, was conducted under terrestrial gravity and did not address the challenges of microgravity phase separation or closed-loop gas management.

The present work advances this concept by: (a) separating the PFC into two independent loops for CO₂ and O₂ transport, (b) introducing a thermal regeneration stage that actively strips CO₂ from PFC at elevated temperature to overcome the low driving force from cabin pCO₂, (c) coupling the PFC loops to hollow-fiber membrane contactors for completely bubble-free gas exchange, and (d) integrating the entire system into a mathematically closed model suitable for preliminary aerospace design.

## 3.5 Hollow-Fiber Membrane Contactors

Hollow-fiber membrane contactors (HFMCs) consist of thousands of microporous capillary tubes potted into a cylindrical shell. The pore size (typically 0.02–0.05 µm) is small enough to prevent liquid penetration due to surface tension (for hydrophobic materials like polypropylene or PTFE), while allowing gas molecules to diffuse freely. This creates a very high interfacial area per unit volume—typically 1,000 to 5,000 m²/m³—far exceeding what is achievable in bubble columns or packed beds [23].

Commercial HFMCs (e.g., Liqui-Cel by 3M) are widely used for degassing water in industrial and semiconductor applications. For ECLSS, HFMCs have been proposed for CO₂ removal from cabin air and for O₂ supply to photobioreactors [5,13]. The mass transfer performance is characterized by the overall mass transfer coefficient K_G (for gas-side limited processes) or k_L (for liquid-side limited processes), with the volumetric coefficient k_L a typically in the range 0.001–0.1 s⁻¹ depending on flow conditions.

A key challenge for space applications is fouling. Algal extracellular polymeric substances, mineral precipitates from the nutrient medium, and biofilm formation can reduce mass transfer coefficients over time. Mitigation strategies include periodic backflushing, ultrasonic cleaning, and the use of anti-fouling coatings. These are incorporated into the FMEA and hardware design.

## 3.6 Summary of Research Gap

The literature establishes the individual feasibility of algal photobioreactors for gas exchange, PFC-mediated CO₂/O₂ transport, HFMC-based bubble-free transfer, and regenerative ECLSS architectures. However, no prior work has integrated all four elements into a mathematically closed, dynamically stable, and hardware-specified system designed explicitly for microgravity auxiliary life support. This report addresses that gap.

# SECTION 4: SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

## 4.1 Overview and Design Philosophy

The Hybrid Algal–PFC Auxiliary ECLSS is designed as a modular, fault-tolerant subsystem that provides partial closure of a crew member's metabolic O₂ and CO₂ loads. The architecture strictly separates the life-critical primary physicochemical ECLSS from the supplementary bioregenerative loop. The auxiliary loop can fail completely without jeopardizing crew survival; the primary system automatically compensates for the lost capacity within its design margins.

Three physically distinct fluid volumes interact through passive membrane contactors:

1. **The Aqueous Photobioreactor (Volume \(V_{aq}\))** – a 20 L stirred-tank reactor containing the algal culture suspended in a defined nutrient medium.
2. **The CO₂ PFC Transport Loop** – a closed liquid circuit circulating approximately 10 L of perfluorodecalin (PFD) through a cabin air contactor, a thermal regenerator, and an injection membrane into the aqueous phase.
3. **The O₂ PFC Transport Loop** – a separate closed liquid circuit, also 10 L of PFD, circulating through a hollow-fiber membrane contactor immersed in the algal culture and a degasser module that releases O₂ to the cabin.

These three volumes are coupled by four membrane modules and a regeneration heater, as shown in the process flow diagram (Figure 1).

**Figure 1: Process Flow Diagram (Text Description)**

```
[Crew Cabin (V_cab = 50 m³)]
    │
    ├─ CO₂ Capture ─────► [CO₂ PFC Loop]
    │   (HFMC, 2.0 m²)      │
    │                        ▼
    │               [Regenerator Heater]
    │                  T = 333 K, η = 0.85
    │                        │
    │                        ▼
    │               [CO₂ Injection HFMC]
    │                  (0.10 m²) ───► [Aqueous Phase (20 L)]
    │                                      │
    │◄────────────── [O₂ Degasser] ◄───────┘
    │                 (2.24 m²)        [O₂ HFMC (0.60 m²)]
    │                                      │
    └──────────────────────────────────────┘
              [O₂ PFC Loop]
```

All gas-liquid and liquid-liquid exchange occurs across microporous hollow-fiber membranes (pore size 0.02–0.05 µm, hydrophobic polypropylene or polymethylpentene). The membranes physically separate the phases, eliminating bubble formation entirely—the key design requirement for microgravity compatibility.

## 4.2 Aqueous Photobioreactor

The algal culture is maintained in a 20 L borosilicate or stainless steel vessel equipped with:

- A water jacket connected to a thermoelectric chiller for temperature control at 25 ± 1°C.
- An optical density sensor (680 nm LED/photodiode) for biomass monitoring.
- A pH electrode (ISFET type) and a dissolved oxygen probe (optical fluorescence).
- Inlet ports for fresh nutrient medium, CO₂-enriched water from the injection membrane, and acid/base titrant for pH control.
- Outlet ports for harvest (removing aged culture) and sampling.
- Internal LED illumination: a custom array of 660 nm and 450 nm LEDs delivering a total incident photon flux density of 400 µmol/(m²·s) at the fiber bundle surface.

Mixing is provided by recirculation through the O₂ HFMC loop and by a low-shear impeller to maintain homogeneous conditions and prevent cell settling.

## 4.3 CO₂ PFC Transport Loop

The CO₂ transport loop consists of four components connected in series:

1. **Cabin Capture Contactor (V_cap_co2):** A hollow-fiber module (2.0 m²) where cabin air flows through the fiber lumens and PFD flows on the shell side. CO₂ diffuses from the cabin (pCO₂ ≈ 0.40 kPa) into the PFD, driven by the partial pressure gradient. The PFC exits at a CO₂ concentration of approximately 0.192 mmol/L (state variable C1c).

2. **Thermal Regenerator:** The CO₂-rich PFC is heated from 298 K to 333 K using a 100 W cartridge heater. At the elevated temperature, the Ostwald solubility coefficient for CO₂ in PFD drops from 1.4 to 0.4, releasing approximately 85% of the dissolved CO₂ into a small gas headspace. The released CO₂ accumulates to a partial pressure of ~12.7 kPa (P_regen). The lean PFC exits at a concentration of ~0.034 mmol/L (C2c).

3. **CO₂ Injection Membrane (V_regen_mem):** The high-concentration CO₂ gas from the regenerator headspace passes through the lumens of a second membrane contactor (0.10 m²), while the aqueous algal medium flows on the shell side. CO₂ diffuses into the medium at a rate of 1.32 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s, matching the capture rate. The gas concentration is governed by P_regen and the hot-side temperature.

4. **PFC Return Line:** The lean PFC from the regenerator (now at C2c ≈ 0.034 mmol/L) is cooled back to 298 K via a heat exchanger integrated with the coolant loop and returns to the capture contactor, completing the cycle.

Circulation is provided by a magnetically-coupled gear pump (5.04 L/min, SS316/PTFE wetted parts). The total PFC inventory in the loop is approximately 10 L, distributed between the contactors and a small reservoir.

## 4.4 O₂ PFC Transport Loop

The O₂ transport loop operates in the opposite direction:

1. **Aqueous-to-PFC HFMC (V_HFMC_O2):** A hollow-fiber module (0.60 m²) immersed in the algal bioreactor. Dissolved O₂ in the aqueous phase (C_aq,O₂ ≈ 1.18 mmol/L) diffuses into the PFD on the shell side. The PFC exits with an O₂ concentration of ~3.78 mmol/L (C2o).

2. **Degasser Module:** The O₂-rich PFC flows through a second contactor (2.24 m²) where cabin air (or a small sweep gas flow) on the lumen side provides a low-O₂ environment. O₂ diffuses out of the PFC and into the cabin atmosphere at a rate of 1.65 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s. The lean PFC exits at ~3.45 mmol/L (C1o) and returns to the aqueous contactor.

3. **PFC Circulation:** A second gear pump (3.0 L/min) drives the loop. Total PFC inventory is 10 L.

Note that the two PFC loops are completely independent; there is no mixing of the CO₂-rich and O₂-rich fluids. This separation prevents the thermodynamic conflict that would arise if a single PFC volume were required to simultaneously absorb CO₂ from a low-pressure source and release it to a high-demand sink.

## 4.5 Control Architecture

Two independent PI controllers regulate the auxiliary loop:

- **Turbidostat (biomass control):** The dilution rate D is adjusted based on the optical density measurement to maintain a biomass setpoint of 1.20 g/L. The manipulated variable is the harvest/peristaltic pump speed. Integral anti-windup clamps limit D to physically realizable values.

- **pH stat (acid/base control):** A titrant (HNO₃ or NaOH) is dosed based on the pH measurement to maintain 6.80. The controller acts on total alkalinity through the U_acid/base term in the TA balance.

The cabin atmosphere is controlled by the primary ECLSS, which is modeled as a proportional controller with gains sufficient to hold pCO₂ at 0.40 kPa and pO₂ at 21.3 kPa in the presence of the auxiliary loop's net fluxes. In normal operation, the primary system handles ~94% of the metabolic load, with the auxiliary loop covering the remaining ~6%. If the auxiliary loop fails and isolation valves close, the primary ECLSS automatically ramps to 100% capacity within ~45 seconds, preventing any hazardous atmospheric excursion (see Section 9 for FMEA).

## 4.6 Thermal Management

Heat is removed from the system at three points: the aqueous reactor jacket, the PFC loop heat exchangers (downstream of the regenerator and pumps), and the LED array. A single recirculating chiller (thermoelectric, 300 W capacity) rejects heat to the habitat thermal bus. In case of chiller failure, a phase-change material (PCM) pack (paraffin wax, melting point 28°C) provides ~2 hours of passive cooling, and a bypass valve activates a finned radiator if the cabin ambient is below the reactor setpoint. Redundant coolant pumps and cross-validated RTD sensors ensure single-fault tolerance.

## 5.1 State Variables and Conservation Principles

The system is described by 14 time-dependent state variables, listed in Table 1. Their evolution is governed by a set of coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs) derived from mass balances on each control volume, combined with algebraic relations for chemical speciation, light attenuation, and controller action.

**Table 1: State Variables**

| Symbol | Description | Units |
|---|---|---|
| \(X\) | Biomass concentration in aqueous phase | g/L |
| \([DIC]\) | Total dissolved inorganic carbon in aqueous phase | mol/L |
| \([TA]\) | Total alkalinity of aqueous phase | mol/L |
| \(C_{aq,O_2}\) | Dissolved oxygen concentration in aqueous phase | mol/L |
| \(C_{1c}\) | CO₂ concentration in PFC at capture contactor exit | mol/L |
| \(C_{2c}\) | CO₂ concentration in PFC at regenerator exit | mol/L |
| \(P_{regen}\) | CO₂ partial pressure in regenerator gas headspace | kPa |

| \(C_{2o}\) | O₂ concentration in PFC at aqueous contactor exit | mol/L |
| \(e_{pH}\) | Integral of pH error (controller state) | pH·s |
| \(e_{X}\) | Integral of biomass error (controller state) | g·s/L |
| \(P_{CO_2,cab}\) | Cabin CO₂ partial pressure | kPa |
| \(P_{O_2,cab}\) | Cabin O₂ partial pressure | kPa |

## 5.2 Carbonate Chemistry and pH

The dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) system is governed by the equilibria:

\[
\text{CO}_{2(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{HCO}_3^- + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{CO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+
\]

with dissociation constants \(K_1 = 4.47 \times 10^{-7}\) and \(K_2 = 4.68 \times 10^{-11}\) at 298 K [21]. The total alkalinity is defined as:

\[
[TA] = [\text{HCO}_3^-] + 2[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] + [\text{OH}^-] - [\text{H}^+]
\]

Given DIC and TA, the hydrogen ion concentration [H⁺] is found by solving the implicit charge balance:

\[
[TA] - \left( \frac{K_1[H^+] + 2K_1K_2}{[H^+]^2 + K_1[H^+] + K_1K_2} \right)[DIC] - \frac{K_w}{[H^+]} + [H^+] = 0
\]

where \(K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\). This equation is solved numerically via the Newton-Raphson method at each time step (or each algebraic iteration). The pH is then:

\[
pH = -\log_{10}[H^+]
\]

The fraction of DIC present as free dissolved CO₂, denoted \(\alpha_0\), is:

\[
\alpha_0 = \left( 1 + \frac{K_1}{[H^+]} + \frac{K_1K_2}{[H^+]^2} \right)^{-1}
\]

Thus, the bioavailable CO₂ concentration is:

\[
C_{aq,CO_2} = \alpha_0 [DIC]
\]

## 5.3 Algal Growth Kinetics

The specific growth rate \(\mu\) (s⁻¹) of *Chlorella vulgaris* is modeled using a multiplicative Monod-type expression with light and CO₂ as limiting substrates:

\[
\mu = \mu_{max} \cdot \left( \frac{I_{avg}}{K_I + I_{avg}} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{C_{aq,CO_2}}{K_C + C_{aq,CO_2}} \right)
\]

where \(\mu_{max} = 2.22 \times 10^{-5}\) s⁻¹ (0.08 h⁻¹), \(K_I = 80\) µmol/(m²·s), and \(K_C = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\) mol/L.

The average light intensity inside the hollow-fiber bundle, accounting for self-shading and inter-fiber screening, is given by the Lambert-Beer integration over a cylindrical bundle of radius \(R_{bundle} = 0.05\) m:

\[
I_{avg} = I_0 \cdot \frac{1 - \exp(-\beta \alpha X R_{bundle})}{\beta \alpha X R_{bundle}}
\]

where \(I_0 = 400\) µmol/(m²·s) is the incident flux at the bundle surface, \(\alpha = 0.18\) m²/g is the specific absorption coefficient, \(\beta = 1.3\) is the inter-fiber shading penalty factor, and \(X\) is the biomass density in g/L (converted to g/m³ internally).

## 5.4 Mass Balances on the Aqueous Phase

The aqueous phase (volume \(V_{aq} = 20\) L) is subject to inflow of fresh medium at dilution rate \(D\) (s⁻¹), removal of spent culture at the same rate, and mass transfer fluxes across the injection membrane (CO₂ in) and the O₂ HFMC (O₂ out).

### Biomass

\[
\frac{dX}{dt} = (\mu - D) X
\]

### Total Inorganic Carbon

\[
\frac{d[DIC]}{dt} = \frac{J_{CO_2,inj}}{V_{aq}} - \frac{\mu X}{Y_{X/CO_2}} - D([DIC] - [DIC]_{feed})
\]

where \(J_{CO_2,inj}\) (mol/s) is the CO₂ injection flux (Section 5.6), and \(Y_{X/CO_2} = 25.0\) g-biomass/mol-CO₂ is the stoichiometric yield. The feed DIC concentration is \([DIC]_{feed} = 1.91 \times 10^{-2}\) mol/L.

### Total Alkalinity

\[
\frac{d[TA]}{dt} = D([TA]_{feed} - [TA]) + 0.15 \cdot \frac{\mu X}{Y_{X/CO_2}} + U_{acid/base}
\]

where \([TA]_{feed} = 6.93 \times 10^{-3}\) mol/L, the term 0.15·(μX/Y) represents alkalinity production from nitrate uptake [17], and \(U_{acid/base}\) is the PI controller output (mol/L·s):

\[
U_{acid/base} = K_{p,pH} (pH_{set} - pH) + K_{i,pH} \int (pH_{set} - pH) dt
\]

with gains \(K_{p,pH} = -8.0 \times 10^{-4}\), \(K_{i,pH} = -3.0 \times 10^{-6}\).

### Dissolved Oxygen

\[
\frac{dC_{aq,O_2}}{dt} = \frac{\mu X}{Y_{X/O_2}} - \frac{J_{O_2,HFMC}}{V_{aq}} - D C_{aq,O_2}
\]

where \(Y_{X/O_2} = Y_{X/CO_2} / PQ = 25.0 / 1.10 = 22.727\) g-biomass/mol-O₂, and \(J_{O_2,HFMC}\) (mol/s) is the O₂ transfer flux to the PFC loop (Section 5.7).

## 5.5 Turbidostat Control Law

The dilution rate is adjusted by a PI controller to maintain the biomass setpoint:

\[
D = D_{nominal} + K_{p,X}(X - X_{set}) + K_{i,X} \int (X - X_{set}) dt
\]

with \(D_{nominal} = 1.68 \times 10^{-5}\) s⁻¹, \(K_{p,X} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\), \(K_{i,X} = 4.0 \times 10^{-7}\). The controller output is clamped to the range \([0, 1.0 \times 10^{-4}]\) s⁻¹ to prevent unphysical negative or excessive dilution.

## 5.6 CO₂ PFC Loop Balances

The CO₂ PFC loop is modeled as three well-mixed compartments: the capture contactor (\(V_{cap} = 2.0\) L), the regenerator (treated as a combined liquid volume \(V_{PFC,CO_2} = 10.0\) L and a gas headspace \(V_{regen,gas} = 0.5\) L), and the return line (lumped into the regenerator liquid volume). The PFC circulates at \(Q_{PFC,CO_2} = 0.084\) L/s.

### Capture Contactor

\[
\frac{dC_{1c}}{dt} = \frac{Q_{PFC,CO_2}}{V_{cap}} (C_{3c} - C_{1c}) + \frac{J_{CO_2,cap}}{V_{cap}}
\]

The capture flux is driven by the gas-liquid partial pressure difference:

\[
J_{CO_2,cap} = K_G A_{cap} \left( P_{CO_2,cab} - \frac{C_{1c}}{L_{CO_2,cold}} RT_{cabin} \right)
\]

where \(K_G A_{cap} = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\) mol/(s·kPa), \(L_{CO_2,cold} = 1.4\), and \(RT_{cabin} = R \cdot 298.15\) K.

### Regenerator (Liquid Phase)

\[
\frac{dC_{2c}}{dt} = \frac{Q_{PFC,CO_2}}{V_{PFC,CO_2}} (C_{1c} - C_{2c}) - \frac{J_{CO_2,release}}{V_{PFC,CO_2}}
\]

The rate of CO₂ release from the heated PFC is:

\[
J_{CO_2,release} = Q_{PFC,CO_2} (C_{1c} - C_{2c})
\]

This reflects the fact that at 333 K the equilibrium solubility is much lower; the PFC exits the regenerator at a concentration \(C_{2c}\) that is substantially lower than \(C_{1c}\). The detailed kinetics of the release are captured by the efficiency parameter \(\eta = 0.85\), but the dynamic equation above uses the actual concentrations and flow rate to compute the mass transfer, with the equilibrium condition \(C_{2c}^{eq} = L_{CO_2,hot} P_{CO_2,cab} / RT_{hot}\) serving as the lower bound; the actual \(C_{2c}\) is determined by the balance of convective removal and release.

### Regenerator Gas Headspace

\[
\frac{dP_{regen}}{dt} = \frac{RT_{hot}}{V_{regen,gas}} \left( J_{CO_2,release} - J_{CO_2,inj} \right)
\]

where \(P_{regen}\) is in kPa, and the factor \(RT_{hot}/V_{regen,gas}\) converts mol/s to kPa/s.

### CO₂ Injection Membrane

\[
J_{CO_2,inj} = k_L a_{regen} V_{regen,memb} \left( \frac{P_{regen}}{RT_{hot}} - C_{aq,CO_2} \right)
\]

with \(k_L a_{regen} = 0.02\) s⁻¹ and \(V_{regen,memb} = 0.5\) L. The term \(P_{regen}/RT_{hot}\) is the gas-phase CO₂ concentration (mol/L) in equilibrium with the headspace. This flux is constrained to be non-negative (no back-diffusion).

### PFC Return

\[
\frac{dC_{3c}}{dt} = \frac{Q_{PFC,CO_2}}{V_{PFC,CO_2}} (C_{2c} - C_{3c})
\]

In the steady-state solution, \(C_{3c} = C_{2c}\), which is consistent with the assumption of a well-mixed return line.

## 5.7 O₂ PFC Loop Balances

The O₂ loop uses a separate PFC volume (\(V_{PFC,O_2} = 10.0\) L) circulating at \(Q_{PFC,O_2} = 0.05\) L/s.

### Aqueous-to-PFC Contactor

\[
\frac{dC_{2o}}{dt} = \frac{Q_{PFC,O_2}}{V_{PFC,O_2}} (C_{1o} - C_{2o}) + \frac{J_{O_2,HFMC}}{V_{PFC,O_2}}
\]

\[
J_{O_2,HFMC} = k_L a_{O_2} V_{HFMC,O_2} \left( C_{aq,O_2} - \frac{C_{2o}}{m_{O_2}} \right)
\]

with \(k_L a_{O_2} = 0.02\) s⁻¹, \(V_{HFMC,O_2} = 3.0\) L, and the PFC/water partition coefficient \(m_{O_2} = 4.2\). The term \(C_{2o}/m_{O_2}\) is the aqueous-phase O₂ concentration that would be in equilibrium with the PFC at concentration \(C_{2o}\). The flux is non-negative.

### Degasser Module

\[
\frac{dC_{1o}}{dt} = \frac{Q_{PFC,O_2}}{V_{PFC,O_2}} (C_{2o} - C_{1o}) - \frac{J_{O_2,degas}}{V_{PFC,O_2}}
\]

\[
J_{O_2,degas} = K_G A_{degas} \left( \frac{C_{1o}}{L_{O_2}} RT_{cabin} - P_{O_2,cab} \right)
\]

where \(K_G A_{degas} = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\) mol/(s·kPa) and \(L_{O_2} = 0.4\).

## 5.8 Cabin Atmosphere Balances

The cabin (volume \(V_{cab} = 50\) m³) receives metabolic CO₂ and consumes O₂ at rates corresponding to one crew member (2.626 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s CO₂ produced, 3.038 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s O₂ consumed). The primary ECLSS acts as a proportional controller:

\[
J_{CO_2,primary} = f_{primary} \cdot \dot{m}_{CO_2,met} + K_{primary,CO_2} (P_{CO_2,cab} - P_{CO_2,set})
\]

\[
J_{O_2,primary} = f_{primary} \cdot \dot{m}_{O_2,met} + K_{primary,O_2} (P_{O_2,set} - P_{O_2,cab})
\]

with \(f_{primary} = 0.939\), \(K_{primary,CO_2} = K_{primary,O_2} = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\) mol/(s·kPa). The cabin partial pressures evolve as:

\[
\frac{dP_{CO_2,cab}}{dt} = \frac{RT_{cabin}}{V_{cab}} \left( \dot{m}_{CO_2,met} - J_{CO_2,primary} - J_{CO_2,cap} \right)
\]

\[
\frac{dP_{O_2,cab}}{dt} = \frac{RT_{cabin}}{V_{cab}} \left( J_{O_2,primary} + J_{O_2,degas} - \dot{m}_{O_2,met} \right)
\]

## 5.9 Steady-State Reduction

For the steady-state analysis, all time derivatives are set to zero. The resulting system of 14 nonlinear algebraic equations was solved using the fsolve algorithm (SciPy) with a Newton-type method. The initial guess was informed by the target setpoints and approximate flux balances. The solution converged to a maximum residual of \(2.82 \times 10^{-17}\), effectively at machine precision. The numerical values of all state variables and fluxes at steady state are reported in Section 6.

# SECTION 7: EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS AND COMPARISON

## 7.1 Performance Metrics of the Auxiliary Loop

From the steady-state solution, the following key performance indicators are derived for the 20 L bioreactor:

- **Specific CO₂ removal rate:** \(1.3249 \times 10^{-5} \, \text{mol/s}\) per 20 L = \(6.62 \times 10^{-7} \, \text{mol/(L·s)}\) = **57.2 mmol/(L·day)**.
- **Specific O₂ production rate:** \(1.6917 \times 10^{-5} \, \text{mol/s}\) per 20 L = \(8.46 \times 10^{-7} \, \text{mol/(L·s)}\) = **73.1 mmol/(L·day)**.
- **Biomass productivity:** \(D \cdot X = 1.602 \times 10^{-5} \, \text{s}^{-1} \times 1.20 \, \text{g/L} \times 86400 \, \text{s/day} = 1.66 \, \text{g/(L·day)}\).
- **Closure fraction (CO₂):** \(1.3249 \times 10^{-5} / 2.626 \times 10^{-4} = 5.04\%\).
- **Closure fraction (O₂):** \(1.6541 \times 10^{-5} / 3.038 \times 10^{-4} = 5.44\%\).

## 7.2 Photosynthetic Efficiency

Photosynthetic efficiency is defined as the ratio of chemical energy stored as biomass to the energy of incident photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). The higher heating value of *Chlorella* dry biomass is approximately 22 kJ/g. The daily biomass productivity of 1.66 g/(L·day) yields an energy storage rate of \(1.66 \times 22 \times 10^3 = 3.65 \times 10^4 \, \text{J/(L·day)}\).

The incident light power per unit reactor volume is:

\[
P_{light} = I_0 \cdot A_{illum} \cdot E_{photon},
\]

where \(I_0 = 400 \, \mu\text{mol/(m}^2\text{·s)} = 4.00 \times 10^{-4} \, \text{mol/(m}^2\text{·s)}\), the illuminated surface area per unit volume is approximately \(2/R_{bundle} = 2 / 0.05 = 40 \, \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3 = 0.04 \, \text{m}^2/\text{L}\), and the average energy per mole of photons in the PAR range (400–700 nm, assuming a typical LED spectrum centered at 660 nm) is \(E_{photon} \approx 2.17 \times 10^5 \, \text{J/mol}\). Hence:

\[
P_{light} = (4.00 \times 10^{-4}) \times 0.04 \times 2.17 \times 10^5 = 3.47 \, \text{J/(L·s)} = 3.00 \times 10^5 \, \text{J/(L·day)}.
\]

Photosynthetic efficiency:

\[
\eta_{photo} = \frac{3.65 \times 10^4}{3.00 \times 10^5} = 0.122 = 12.2\%.
\]

This value is at the upper end of the reported range for continuous microalgal photobioreactors (5–15% [16]) and is consistent with the model assumption of optimized light delivery via fiber optics and the absence of photoinhibition at moderate I₀. However, the efficiency is calculated based on incident PAR only; accounting for electrical-to-light conversion losses in LEDs (~40% efficiency) would reduce the overall energy efficiency to approximately \(12.2\% \times 0.40 = 4.9\%\) relative to electrical input.

## 7.3 Comparison with ISS Physicochemical ECLSS

The ISS Oxygen Generation Assembly (OGA) electrolyzes water to produce O₂ with a power consumption of approximately 420 W for a 3–4 crew load, corresponding to about 2.9 kg O₂/day. The electrochemical efficiency is around 50%, giving a specific O₂ production rate of roughly **8 g O₂ per watt-day**. The Sabatier reactor recovers about 50% of the oxygen from CO₂, resulting in a net water recovery rate of ~50%. The CDRA adsorbs CO₂ onto zeolite beds that are regenerated by exposure to vacuum; this process does not recover carbon and requires periodic bed replacement (approximately every 1–2 years under continuous use).

Our auxiliary loop produces O₂ at a rate of 46.8 g/day (Table 3) for a total electrical power consumption estimated at ~150 W (LEDs ~100 W, pumps ~30 W, regenerator ~20 W averaged). The specific O₂ production rate is 46.8/150 = **0.31 g O₂ per watt-day**—approximately 26 times lower than electrolysis. However, the bioregenerative loop accomplishes simultaneous CO₂ removal and carbon fixation without consumable sorbents and without venting methane. Its value is therefore not in energy efficiency but in carbon loop closure and consumable mass reduction on long missions.

## 7.4 Comparison with MELiSSA Pilot Plant

The MELiSSA pilot plant in Barcelona operates a *Spirulina platensis* photobioreactor with a working volume of approximately 100 L, achieving a biomass productivity of 0.5–1.0 g/(L·day) under artificial illumination [4,13]. The reported photosynthetic efficiency is on the order of 6% (PAR basis). Our predicted productivity of 1.66 g/(L·day) is higher, which may reflect the enhanced gas transfer provided by the PFC loops (reducing CO₂ limitation) and the optimized pH control at 6.80 that maximizes dissolved CO₂ availability. However, the MELiSSA system benefits from direct natural sunlight in some configurations, which dramatically reduces electrical power needs. Direct comparison is complicated by the different algal strains, reactor geometries, and illumination strategies.

The specific CO₂ removal rate of the MELiSSA *Spirulina* compartment is approximately 25–40 mmol/(L·day) based on published data; our calculated value of 57.2 mmol/(L·day) is notably higher, a direct consequence of operating at low pH (6.80 vs. 9–10 for *Spirulina*) where the fraction of DIC present as CO₂(aq) is much larger.

## 7.5 Scaling Considerations

The auxiliary loop processes only ~5% of one crew member's metabolic load with a 20 L reactor. Extrapolating linearly, a 100% closure for a crew of four would require approximately \(4 \times 20 / 0.05 = 1600\) L of reactor volume and an illuminated surface area of ~64 m². This is comparable to the total pressurized volume of a small habitat module and would demand ~12 kW of electrical power for lighting alone. Such a system is unlikely to be feasible for early Mars missions given mass and power constraints.

However, the auxiliary loop is not designed for 100% closure; its purpose is to provide a small but continuously operating bioregenerative supplement that reduces the metabolic load on the primary system, extends consumable lifetimes, and serves as a technology demonstrator for future, more efficient bio-engineered systems. A 5% reduction in CDRA sorbent mass and OGA water consumption over a 3-year mission translates to hundreds of kilograms of saved consumables.

# SECTION 8: HARDWARE DESIGN AND BILL OF MATERIALS

## 8.1 Design Requirements

The hardware design is derived directly from the steady-state solution (Section 6). All components must satisfy the following requirements:

- **Microgravity compatibility:** No buoyancy-dependent processes; gas-liquid separation achieved via hydrophobic membranes or centrifugal force.
- **Chemical compatibility:** All wetted materials resistant to perfluorodecalin (PFD), aqueous nutrient medium at pH 6.0–7.5, and dilute HNO₃/NaOH titrants.
- **Biological compatibility:** Materials contacting algal culture must be non-toxic, sterilizable, and resistant to biofilm formation.
- **Thermal stability:** Maintain aqueous phase at 25 ± 1°C; prevent PFC thermal degradation (PFD stable to >400°C, but pump seals and sensors must tolerate 60°C regenerator outlet).
- **Single-fault tolerance:** No single component failure (pump, sensor, valve) shall cause culture loss or hazard to crew. Primary ECLSS remains independent.
- **Modularity:** Individual membrane modules, pumps, and sensors must be isolatable and replaceable.

# SECTION 9: FAILURE MODE AND EFFECTS ANALYSIS

## 9.1 Scope and Methodology

A Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is conducted for the auxiliary ECLSS loop to identify potential failure modes, assess their severity and likelihood, and define automatic and crew-level responses that prevent hazardous cabin atmosphere excursions. The analysis follows the framework established by NASA for ISS payload safety reviews [24] and considers three severity classes:

- **Class I (Catastrophic):** Failure that could cause loss of crew or loss of mission. Not acceptable under any single fault.
- **Class II (Critical):** Failure that degrades life support performance beyond operational limits without immediate crew hazard if corrected within a specified time.
- **Class III (Marginal):** Failure that reduces auxiliary loop performance but does not impact crew safety or primary ECLSS function.

Because the auxiliary loop is designed to be non-critical—the primary physicochemical ECLSS remains fully capable of sustaining the crew—most auxiliary loop failures are Class II or III. Only failures that could release toxic substances into the cabin or cause fire are Class I.

## 9.3 Transient Response to Isolation Event (F1, F2)

The most severe auxiliary loop failure is a complete loss of biological function (culture crash or thermal kill). In this event, the automatic response sequence is:

1. **T = 0 s:** OD sensor detects >30% drop from setpoint, OR aqueous RTD exceeds 35°C.
2. **T = 2 s:** Microcontroller confirms alarm condition from redundant sensor.
3. **T = 5 s:** Solenoid isolation valves close on CO₂ capture, CO₂ injection, O₂ HFMC, and O₂ degasser modules. PFC loops continue circulating internally to prevent stagnation.
4. **T = 10 s:** Nutrient feed and harvest pumps stop. Acid/base dosing stops. LED array dims to 10% (maintenance light to preserve seed bank viability if temperature permits).
5. **T = 15 s:** Notification sent to crew via dashboard alarm and auditory signal.
6. **T = 45 s:** Primary ECLSS controller detects the small reduction in net CO₂ removal (approximately 1.32 × 10⁻⁵ mol/s, or 5% of metabolic load) and compensates by increasing CDRA cycle frequency and OGA production rate. The cabin pCO₂ increase during the 45-second transient is bounded by:

\[
\Delta p_{CO_2} \approx \frac{1.32 \times 10^{-5} \, \text{mol/s} \times 45 \, \text{s} \times RT_{cabin}}{V_{cab}} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-4} \, \text{kPa}
\]

This is three orders of magnitude below the 0.42 kPa operational limit and is undetectable by cabin sensors.

The sequence demonstrates that loss of the auxiliary loop poses zero risk of crew hazard and that the primary ECLSS absorbs the additional load well within its design margins.

## 9.4 Contamination and Toxic Release Mitigation

PFCs are generally non-toxic and biologically inert [6]. However, prolonged heating above 300°C (well above the regenerator setpoint of 60°C) can theoretically degrade PFD to produce trace perfluoroisobutylene (PFIB), a respiratory irritant. The regenerator heater includes an independent thermal fuse that cuts power irreversibly at 100°C, preventing any approach to degradation temperatures. Additionally, the regenerator gas outlet line passes through a small activated carbon filter that scrubs any potential volatile byproducts before the gas enters the injection membrane or cabin.

Algal culture sterilization (autoclave at 121°C for 20 minutes prior to inoculation) and sterile medium handling prevent the introduction of pathogenic microorganisms. The culture medium contains no substances hazardous to crew; in the event of a reactor breach, the small volume (20 L) is contained within the secondary enclosure.

# SECTION 10: LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

## 10.1 Modeling Limitations

The present model, while comprehensive, contains several idealizations that must be recognized:

1. **Constant metabolic load:** Crew CO₂ production and O₂ consumption are assumed constant at average resting rates. In reality, metabolic rates vary with activity, sleep cycles, and health status. A robust design would size the system for peak loads or include buffering capacity.

2. **Perfect mixing assumption:** The aqueous reactor is modeled as a continuously stirred tank. In practice, dead zones, wall growth, and non-uniform light distribution create spatial gradients that reduce effective productivity.

3. **No membrane fouling dynamics:** The model assumes constant mass transfer coefficients. Long-term operation invariably leads to biofilm formation and inorganic scaling, which reduce \(k_La\) and \(K_GA\) over time. The FMEA addresses fouling via backflush and module replacement, but the model does not predict the time course of degradation.

4. **Single-species assumption:** The algal culture is assumed axenic. In practice, bacterial contaminants can compete for nutrients, alter pH, and produce inhibitory metabolites. Sterile technique and periodic culture health checks are essential.

5. **Simplified PFC regeneration kinetics:** The regenerator is modeled as an equilibrium stage with a fixed efficiency η. Actual CO₂ release rates depend on bubble nucleation dynamics, heat transfer rates, and PFC residence time distribution. Experimental characterization of a specific regenerator geometry is required to validate η = 0.85.

6. **No nitrogen or phosphorus limitation:** The Monod model includes only light and CO₂ as limiting substrates. In continuous operation, nitrogen, phosphorus, or trace metal limitation can become growth-limiting. The model assumes sufficient nutrient supply via the feed medium.

## 10.2 Experimental Validation Priorities

Before flight qualification, the following experimental milestones should be achieved:

1. **Bench-scale membrane module characterization:** Measure \(k_La\) and \(K_GA\) for each contactor under the exact flow rates, temperatures, and fluid compositions specified. Compare to vendor data and model assumptions.

2. **Regenerator performance mapping:** Operate the regenerator at varying PFC flow rates, heater power, and gas headspace volumes to measure the achieved η and Pregen. Fit a semi-empirical model.

3. **Long-duration algal continuous culture (30–90 days):** Run the aqueous reactor alone (with direct CO₂ sparging and membrane oxygenation) to establish baseline productivity, nutrient consumption rates, and culture stability.

4. **Integrated PFC loop test:** Connect the CO₂ capture, regenerator, and injection modules and operate with a simulated cabin gas stream. Verify that the net CO₂ transfer matches model predictions.

5. **Full-system closed-loop test:** Operate the entire auxiliary ECLSS for a minimum of 30 days with a simulated crew metabolic load (controlled CO₂ injection and O₂ extraction). Monitor all state variables, fluxes, and controller performance. Measure actual closure fractions.

6. **Microgravity compatibility test:** Perform reduced-gravity parabolic flight campaigns or use a clinostat to verify that the membrane contactors function without gas accumulation and that the centrifugal separators effectively manage any entrained gas.

## 10.3 Design Improvements for Future Iterations

1. **Genetic strain optimization:** Select or engineer *Chlorella* strains with higher photosynthetic efficiency, lower light saturation constants, or the ability to secrete lipids (reducing biomass harvest requirements). Synthetic biology approaches could enable direct secretion of O₂ or consumption of CO₂ at higher rates.

2. **Natural illumination for surface missions:** For lunar or Martian bases, concentrated solar light delivered via fiber optics could dramatically reduce electrical power requirements and increase closure fractions. The PFC transport loops would remain essential for bubble-free gas management even under natural illumination.

3. **Higher plants integration:** The algal auxiliary loop could be positioned as the gas exchange engine within a larger bioregenerative system that includes higher plants for food production, with the algae providing fine-tuning of atmospheric composition.

4. **Advanced membrane materials:** Graphene oxide or mixed-matrix membranes with higher CO₂/O₂ selectivity could improve the driving force for gas transfer and reduce module size.

5. **In-situ resource utilization (ISRU):** On Mars, the CO₂-rich atmosphere (95% CO₂ at ~0.6 kPa) could be used directly as the carbon source for the algal reactor, bypassing the cabin capture loop entirely and dramatically increasing closure potential.

## 10.4 Path to Flight Qualification

For eventual deployment on a crewed mission, the prototype must undergo:

- **Environmental testing:** Vibration (6–9 g rms random), thermal vacuum (−20°C to +60°C), and electromagnetic compatibility per NASA GEVS-7000 or equivalent ESA standards.
- **Materials certification:** All materials in contact with cabin atmosphere must pass offgassing tests (ASTM E595) to ensure volatile organic compound levels remain below Spacecraft Maximum Allowable Concentrations (SMACs).
- **Software safety:** The control software must be developed to Level B or C criticality standards depending on the final hazard classification.
- **Redundancy verification:** Fault injection testing must demonstrate that all single-point failures are detected, isolated, and recovered without crew hazard.

These activities are beyond the scope of the present preliminary design but represent the necessary trajectory from prototype to flight hardware.

# SECTION 11: CONCLUSIONS

A complete preliminary design for a Hybrid Algal–Perfluorocarbon Auxiliary ECLSS has been presented, addressing the fundamental challenge of bubble-free gas exchange for bioregenerative life support in microgravity. The principal conclusions are:

1. **Mathematical closure is achievable.** The 14-equation steady-state model, coupling algal growth kinetics, carbonate chemistry, PFC gas transport, thermal regeneration, and cabin atmosphere dynamics, admits a physically consistent solution with all setpoints simultaneously satisfied. The maximum residual of \(2.82 \times 10^{-17}\) across all conservation equations confirms that no hidden contradictions exist in the architecture.

2. **The steady state is dynamically stable.** Integration of the full ODE system from the algebraic solution produces no detectable drift over 72 hours, confirming that the PI turbidostat and pH-stat controllers, combined with the intrinsic negative feedback from growth kinetics and gas transfer, are sufficient to maintain the operating point.

3. **The PFC thermal regeneration loop solves the CO₂ transfer bottleneck.** By heating the PFC carrier from 298 K to 333 K, the Ostwald solubility coefficient for CO₂ drops from 1.4 to 0.4, releasing approximately 85% of the captured CO₂ into a concentrated gas stream suitable for membrane injection into the aqueous phase. This active pumping mechanism overcomes the thermodynamic limitation that prevents passive transfer from cabin pCO₂ levels (0.40 kPa) to the higher CO₂ concentrations required by active algal cultures.

4. **The auxiliary loop processes ~5% of a crew member's metabolic load per 20 L of reactor volume.** While modest, this closure fraction is sufficient to demonstrate feasibility and provide meaningful consumable mass savings on long-duration missions. Scaling to higher closure fractions is possible with larger reactor volumes and higher light intensities, subject to mass and power constraints.

5. **The design is inherently safe.** The auxiliary loop is architecturally isolated from crew survival. Complete failure of the bioregenerative subsystem results in a cabin pCO₂ transient of less than 0.0003 kPa, well within the operational margin of the primary physicochemical ECLSS. All identified failure modes have defined automatic responses and crew contingency actions.

6. **Photosynthetic efficiency of 12.2% (PAR basis)** is predicted under the design illumination conditions, consistent with literature values for optimized *Chlorella* photobioreactors. The specific O₂ production rate is 0.31 g O₂ per watt-day of electrical power, approximately 26 times lower than electrolysis, confirming that the value proposition of bioregenerative ECLSS lies in carbon closure and consumable elimination rather than energy efficiency.

7. **A complete hardware specification and cost estimate** have been developed. The laboratory prototype is estimated at $17,600 (2026 USD) using research-grade components, with a path identified toward flight qualification.

The present work establishes the theoretical and engineering foundation for the next phase of development: experimental validation of the membrane modules, regenerator, and integrated system under controlled laboratory conditions. Successful demonstration would position the hybrid algal-PFC architecture as a viable candidate for the auxiliary bioregenerative loop in future long-duration crewed missions to the Moon and Mars.
